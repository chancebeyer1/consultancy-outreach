# Writing rules (apply to EVERY piece of copy — emails, DMs, connect notes, comments, posts)

These are house rules from the operator's editing standard (2026-08-23). They apply on top of
the campaign Style guide and the task prompt. When a rule here conflicts with a decorative
instinct, this file wins. When it conflicts with the operator's own template shape, the
template wins — these rules govern the WORDS inside the template, not its structure.

## Make verbs do the work

- "I get therapy practices onto insurance panels" NOT "I run a service that gets therapy
  practices onto insurance panels". Cut "I run a service that", "I've built a thing that",
  "we provide a solution that" — name the action.
- "decided" not "made a decision". "can" not "has the ability to".

## The portability test (the single most useful check)

If a sentence could move unchanged to another person, company, or industry, it is filler.
Cut it or replace it with a fact, number, mechanism, or consequence specific to THIS recipient.

- ❌ "slow credentialing probably costs your clients time" (true of anyone, hedged, says nothing)
- ✅ "your clients wait three to six months before they can bill"

## Be concrete

Names, numbers, dates, and mechanisms beat abstractions. "cut deploy time from 40 minutes to 4"
beats "improved efficiency". Never invent a number to satisfy this rule — if there is no real
detail in the payload, write the shorter honest sentence instead.

## Banned words

delve, foster, leverage, utilize, facilitate, empower, streamline, robust, cutting-edge,
paradigm shift, game changer, transformative, elevate, embark, supercharge, harness,
ever-evolving, realm, tapestry, meticulous, intricate, paramount, multifaceted.

## Banned phrases

it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end of the day, when it comes to, at its core,
in today's world, in the age of, the reality is, the truth is, in terms of, going forward,
let's dive in.

## Empty adverbs

Cut just, literally, honestly, simply, actually, truly, fundamentally, importantly, crucially
WHEN THEY ADD NOTHING. Keep one if it carries real emphasis or contrast ("so they can actually
bill" earns it — they genuinely cannot bill before paneling).

## Banned patterns

- **Binary contrasts.** "It's not X, it's Y" / "The question isn't X, it's Y" / "not just X but
  Y". State Y directly.
- **Throat-clearing openers.** "Here's the thing", "Let me be clear", "I'll be honest",
  "The uncomfortable truth is". Delete and state the point.
- **Faux-insight setups.** "What most people get wrong", "here's what nobody tells you",
  "the part everyone misses". Make the claim stand alone.
- **Colon reveals.** "The detail that makes it work: a second agent grades it." Write it as a
  plain sentence. Colons are for lists and labels, not drama.
- **Superficial analysis.** Trailing -ing clauses that pretend to explain: "highlighting",
  "underscoring", "reflecting", "showcasing". Say the consequence instead.
- **Importance puffery.** "marks a pivotal moment", "plays a vital role", "stands as a
  testament", "underscores its significance". State the fact; let the reader judge.
- **Telling the reader what to notice.** "This distinction matters", "the key point is",
  "as you can see", "in other words". If it's clear, delete. If it isn't, add a fact.
- **Weasel attribution.** "experts agree", "studies show", "many argue", "industry reports
  suggest". Name the source or drop the claim. Never invent a source.
- **Synonym cycling.** If "agent" is the right word, say "agent" every time. Don't rotate to
  "assistant", "tool", "system" for variety.
- **Negative listing.** "Not a chatbot. Not a wrapper. A real system." Just say what it is.
- **Dramatic fragmentation.** "X. And Y. And Z." / "That's it. That's the whole thing."
- **Rhetorical setups.** "What if I told you", "Think about it:", "Plot twist:", and
  question-then-answer pairs you ask yourself.
- **Fake-profound kickers.** No closing aphorism, metaphor, or mic-drop line. End on the last
  concrete sentence.
- **Summary endings.** No "In conclusion", "Ultimately", "Overall", or a recap of a message the
  reader just read.

## Punctuation and formatting

- **No em or en dashes, and no "--".** Use a comma, a period, or parentheses.
- No emoji in headings. No bold mid-sentence for emphasis. No bullet lists where two sentences
  read better. Format follows content.
- Active voice. Never let an inanimate thing perform a human verb ("the decision emerged").

## What NOT to strip

Preserve the operator's real voice: contractions, plain words, a blunt opinion, a short
sentence that lands. Keep "Feel free to reply or text me at <phone>. Thanks" verbatim when the
Style guide supplies a number — that is the operator's own sign-off, not filler. Do not
professionalize a sentence that already sounds like a person talking.
